About

About

Luca and Camilla Corona met in Tibet in the 1980s, as the country cautiously opened to outsiders. They were quickly captivated by the rugged landscape, the people, the Buddhist culture, and the Tibetans’ distinctive artistic flair.

Luca, a guide fluent in Tibetan and a student of Buddhist philosophy, traveled extensively throughout the region. These journeys revealed not only the remarkable art but also the fragility of what remained: decades of upheaval, deliberate destruction, and recent consumerism had stripped many homes and religious sites of their treasures.

Together they began collecting antique furniture to showcase a lesser known aspect of Tibetan culture. Only a few pieces arriving from the Tibetan countryside to Lhasa were ready to place in one’s home, so everything had to be carefully cleaned and inspected. In the early 1990s, while living in Kathmandu, they established a small workshop with Tibetan craftsmen to restore each piece using traditional methods. Metal nails added over the years were removed and replaced with traditional joinery — wooden pegs and “butterfly” joints. Modern synthetic varnish used by the dealers in Tibet was gently stripped away and the surfaces restored and finished with traditional shellac polish. Over three decades they enhanced their collection of carefully conserved pieces; many of which were used in catalogues and displayed around the world including in the landmark Pacific Asia Museum exhibition in California.

Kathmandu remained the centre of the Tibetan furniture trade through the 1990s and early 2000s. In 2012, Luca and Camilla opened the doors to their newly built eco-resort on the northern rim of the Kathmandu Valley directly under the Shivapuri forest. The guest house, built in Nepali style, was the perfect setting to display local art, handmade Himalayan rugs and antique Tibetan furniture.

Background

History and Influences

An event that completely transformed Tibetan culture was the introduction of Buddhism as the state religion in the 7th Century by Emperor Songsten Gampo and his two foreign wives: the Licchavi Princess Bhrikuti from Nepal, and the Tang Princess Wencheng from China. Buddhism had a profound effect on all aspects of society and culture, including the use and function of Tibetan furniture.

Another influential factor was the availability of wood, a primary resource. Extensive forests covered the Eastern region of Kham and the province of Kongpo prior to the Chinese occupation and subsequent massive deforestation. However, most of Central Tibet and the northern plain of Chang Tang lie above 3,500 metres in altitude, so the type of timber available was limited to a white, soft poplar, known as chapa, or the more hard-wearing spruce, pine and fir which had to be transported at considerable cost from the Southern district of Lhoka.

The Silk Road

One of the main routes on the Silk Road skirted just North of the Tibetan Plateau, while minor caravan routes made their way up from Xining in China across the northern plain to Lhasa, thus linking Tibet with Central Asia and beyond. A further ancient route, known as the Tea Horse Road, wound through the mountains of Sichuan and Yunnan onto the Tibetan plateau, taking tea to Tibet and ponies back to China.

To the West, Leh in Ladakh was a crossroads for caravans between Tibet, Kashmir and Yarkand. Routes crossing the mighty Himalayas linked Lhasa with Nepal and the Indian subcontinent. For centuries, these routes enabled the transmission of goods, skills, knowledge, culture and beliefs — as well as the exchange of artistic methods and tastes.

Tibetan Style

Tibet absorbed outside influences in the form of religion from India and aesthetics from Kashmir, Nepal, Central Asia and China, but in the process created works of art that were unquestionably Tibetan. Religious art predominated. Entering a Tibetan monastery, one is struck by the kaleidoscopic colours, textures and design. The walls are covered from top to bottom with murals of a vast array of Buddhas, Bodhisattvas, Arhats and their deeds or life stories. Immense gold statues dressed in silken robes, encrusted with jewels are flanked by carved and painted altars. Cabinets and long tables depicting goddesses are piled high with offerings and flickering butter lamps. Even the ceilings are alive with wonderful colours and auspicious images.

A thangka painter who depicted Buddhist deities, scenes or mandalas had to follow strict iconographic rules according to Buddhist texts, while a decorator or tson pa who was responsible for painting furniture, architectural features and interiors was free to use his skill to combine any popular motif in a fresh and artistic manner, with particular attention given to the use of colour and shading. Although the freedom of a tson pa was seemingly limitless, a definite Tibetan style emerged in the manner in which they executed the recurring motifs and observed some unwritten conventions.

The Tibetan Furniture Market

For centuries, Lhasa was an important trading centre between the Indian subcontinent to the South, China to the East, and Central Asia to the North-West. This trade came to an abrupt halt with the Chinese occupation of Tibet in the 1950s. Given the extensive destruction of Tibetan cultural heritage that took place, it is surprising that anything survived at all. The destruction of the monasteries and temples began in Amdo and Kham as early as 1958, long before the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976). The dismantling was systematic; first the valuable golden statues and moveable art works were removed and sent to China, then furniture and other items considered ‘utilitarian’ were distributed locally amongst the general population, while sacred scriptures were burnt.

The Tibetans did what they could to preserve the remaining physical vestiges of their culture, including the monastic furniture in their possession. The first furniture to arrive in Kathmandu in the late 1980s was in surprisingly good condition. Gradually, given the increased demand, Tibetan dealers collected blackened pieces from far-flung villages and cleaned off the soot that often covered the entire piece, and then applied a thick, unnatural synthetic varnish to replace the shine.

The First Exhibition

The first ever exhibition of Tibetan furniture was held at the Pacific Asia Museum in Pasadena, California between November 2004 and February 2005. Luca Corona was asked to assess the pieces and write the core article detailing how the furniture was made and its use in Tibet prior to 1950 titled Tibetan Furniture: Construction, Form and Function which was published in the book-catalogue for the exhibition called Wooden Wonders: Tibetan Furniture in Secular and Religious Life edited by David Kamansky.

A Personal Memory

Every collector knows the excitement of entering an Aladdin’s cave filled with hidden treasures and intriguing finds. Stepping into the house of a local dealer offered a unique glimpse into Tibetan culture and everyday life.

Luca and Camilla clearly recall their anticipation of entering a Lhasa dealer’s home, adjusting to the darkness after the bright mountain sunlight, and being led through the sombre corridor to the main room. The smells of incense, butter lamps, and momos (Tibetan dumplings) would waft through the air as they sat on the wooden couch, covered with a beautiful, dusty old rug. The lady of the house would enter with a thermos of steaming Tibetan butter tea, the perfect energy drink for high altitudes.

Settling into this increasingly familiar environment, they caught up with their hosts on family news, the current situation, and the latest dealer gossip: who had bought what from whom, for how much, and who had overpaid for an infamous fake. Meanwhile, their eyes were roving around the room, glimpsing a perfect pair of old cabinets, a delightful table, often still covered in soot, or a precious old teapot placed casually in front of them.

During these encounters, they would look around with a feigned air of indifference while the dealer, usually a Khampa from Eastern Tibet, declared how much he paid for his latest acquisitions. After to-ing and fro-ing and often some well-practiced theatrics, they would remind him of their reputation for prompt payment and the deal would close.
